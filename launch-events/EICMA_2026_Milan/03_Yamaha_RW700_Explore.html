Yamaha RW700 Explore

Yamaha puts the Ténéré's 689cc twin and its clutchless Y-AMT transmission into a machine with a floorboard, under-seat storage and a keyless knurled main switch — deleting the clutch lever and the gear pedal to invent a category between scooter and adventure bike.

01 · Design Highlights

Adventure proportions on a step-through logic.

Left profile on location · Yamaha
TANK-SHAPED UPPER BODY

A tall bike with a low middle

In profile the machine carries full adventure-bike height and screen, but the section between seat and footboard drops right away. That dip is the whole proposition: the rider steps through rather than over, while the eye still reads a full-size machine.

Frontal elevation · Yamaha
HAND GUARDS AND SCREEN

A face assembled from ADV parts

Tall screen, hand guards, twin lamps and a knobbly front tyre are the adventure segment's agreed signals, and Yamaha uses all four. The frontal view is doing category work: it tells a buyer which shelf this belongs on before any spec is read.

Rear three-quarter · Yamaha
FLAT REAR DECK

A tail drawn as a load platform

From above and behind, the tail resolves into a broad flat deck rather than a tapered seat unit. Giving the rear a horizontal plane is what makes the luggage conversation possible later, and it is a decision taken at the styling stage.

Flank and graphics · Yamaha
RW700 FLANK GRAPHIC

The model name set on the flank

The RW700 mark sits on the body's widest panel with the tuning-fork badge above it. Naming a brand-new series prominently on the side is how a manufacturer tells the market that this is a platform, not a one-off variant of something else.

02 · Technology / Product Architecture

Two controls deleted, two volumes gained.

Y-AMT actuator cover · Yamaha
Y-AMT ACTUATOR COVER

The gearbox actuator, named on its lid

A cast cover embossed Y-AMT bolts to the engine's upper side. The automated transmission is not hidden inside the cases; it is an identifiable add-on module with its own housing, which is what lets one engine serve both manual and clutchless machines.

Centre stand and final drive · Yamaha
EXPOSED CHAIN DRIVE

A chain, not a belt, under a floorboard

Despite the step-through layout, final drive is an exposed chain and the machine carries a centre stand to service it. A scooter would use a belt and hide it; keeping the chain is a maintenance cost accepted in exchange for the engine it lets you fit.

Engine and skid plate · Yamaha
SILVER SUMP SKID PLATE

A plate under the lowest point

A silver skid plate sits beneath the sump, finished bright rather than black. Making the protection visible is a claim about intent: it says the low-slung architecture is expected to meet the ground, not merely that it might survive doing so.

Under-seat storage · Yamaha
DEEP UNDER-SEAT BUCKET

Storage where a fuel tank would be

Hinging the seat reveals a deep moulded bucket. On a conventional motorcycle this volume is tank; moving fuel elsewhere to free it is the single packaging decision that most distinguishes this machine from the Ténéré it shares an engine with.

03 · Aesthetic Analysis

Matte green, machined silver, one warm accent.

Left switch cube · Yamaha
FOUR CONTROL TYPES

Four kinds of switch on one cube

A rocker, a round button, a four-way joystick with a centre press and a separate finger paddle share one housing. Each has a different shape and action, so a gloved thumb can distinguish them by feel rather than by reading the legends.

Stepped seat and tail · Yamaha
TWO-PIECE STEPPED SEAT

A step that states where to sit

The saddle is split into two levels with a defined step between them. On a machine whose whole middle is open, that step is the only thing fixing the rider's fore-aft position, so it does an ergonomic job as well as a visual one.

Fork and front end · Yamaha
GOLD FORK AGAINST GREEN

Gold legs doing all the contrast

Against matte Forest Green bodywork and black everything else, the gold fork tubes are the only warm element in the whole machine. Putting the one high-chroma finish on the suspension reads as a performance claim without a single decal saying so.

Under a concrete underpass · Yamaha
URBAN ACTION SETTING

Shot in the city, not the dunes

Yamaha's action photography is set under concrete underpasses and along tree-lined city avenues rather than out on sand. The static shots sell the adventure silhouette, while the riding shots quietly admit where this machine will actually spend its working week.

05 · What Zebra Can Apply

Put the mode set around the control.

Keyless main switch · Yamaha01

Print the states on the ring

Every position this knob can reach is engraved around it, so the control teaches itself. Zebra publishes no rotary or detented control; if one is added, print its full state set on the housing.

Power delivery mode page · Yamaha02

Draw what the setting changes

The mode page highlights the engine in red on a drawing of the machine, so a setting points at its subsystem. Show the part being changed, then test whether fewer workers pick the wrong profile.

Riding data and a call, one layout · Yamaha03

Hold one screen grammar

Riding data and an incoming call share the same layout rules. Zebra's Enterprise Home Screen config lives on each device and cannot be cloned between them; define the grammar at family level.

Riding posture, side-on · Yamaha04

Design the resting posture

Deleting the gear pedal let Yamaha give the foot a flat platform instead of a peg, so the default position is designed. Specify where a worker's hand rests between scans, then measure grip fatigue.

06 · Key Takeaway

Deleting two controls opened a new category.

Removing the clutch lever and the gear pedal is what frees the left switch cube, the foot platform and the volume under the seat — Yamaha redrew the body around two absences rather than adding automation to an old layout. For Zebra the question is which input could be deleted, and what the hand gets instead.
